# Analysts, Information & Microstructure

How sell-side behavior and information gaps create exploitable patterns. Load as a **sub-path of
Path 2** when the thesis turns on consensus, forecasts, or information asymmetry.

## 1. Scharfstein & Stein (1990) — Herd Behavior and Investment

**Mechanism:** Agents (including sell-side analysts) **herd** because of career risk. Being wrong
alongside the crowd is safe; being wrong alone is career-ending. So analysts cluster their forecasts
and ratings, even when private information suggests otherwise. Consensus reflects conformity as much
as conviction.

**Application checklist:**
- Treat tight consensus skeptically: is it clustered because the answer is obvious, or because no
  one wants to break from the pack?
- Look for names where career-risk herding has suppressed dispersion — a clustered consensus that is
  vulnerable to a single credible defector is where the trade is.
- The most asymmetric setups: when you have a defensible non-consensus view and understand *why* the
  crowd is afraid to hold it.

**What it changes:** Reframes "consensus" from a signal of truth into a signal of conformity,
identifying where consensus is the trade rather than the constraint.

**Failure modes:**
- Mistaking herded consensus for validated truth.
- Going non-consensus without understanding why consensus is stuck (you may just be wrong).

## 2. Abarbanell & Bernard (1992) — Analyst Self-Selection Bias and Mean Reversion in EPS Forecasts

**Mechanism:** Analysts **under-react to recent earnings information** — they don't fully
incorporate the latest surprise into their next forecast, so forecast errors are predictable and
serially correlated. A persistent, exploitable bias in the consensus number itself.

**Application checklist:**
- After a surprise, ask whether consensus estimates have fully caught up or are lagging.
- Lagging estimates → estimate revisions are coming → a re-rating catalyst (links to Ball & Brown drift / PEAD).
- Build your own forward number and compare to consensus; the gap, if defensible, is the edge.

**What it changes:** Identifies estimate-revision momentum as a catalyst and quantifies the gap
between your number and a lagging consensus.

**Failure modes:**
- Assuming consensus has fully priced the latest quarter (it usually lags).
- Anchoring your own forecast to the stale consensus you are trying to beat.

## 3. Bradshaw, Richardson & Sloan (2001) — Do Analysts and Auditors Use Information in Accruals?

**Mechanism:** Extends Sloan (1996). Analysts (and auditors) **fail to penalize high-accrual firms**
— their forecasts don't reflect the predictable reversal of accruals, and they are most optimistic
exactly when accruals are highest. A persistent blind spot, which is *why* the accruals anomaly
survives.

**Application checklist:**
- Cross-reference the accrual ratio (from accounting-quality.md) against consensus optimism.
- High accruals + bullish consensus = the setup where the market is most exposed and the short/
  fade is cleanest.
- This is the mechanism that keeps Sloan's anomaly alive — the gatekeepers don't price it.

**What it changes:** Turns the Sloan accrual flag into an actionable consensus-fade thesis by
confirming the crowd is on the wrong side.

**Failure modes:**
- Spotting high accruals but not checking whether consensus has (rarely) already adjusted.
- Forgetting that this blind spot is exactly what makes the trade durable.

## 4. Akerlof (1970) — The Market for Lemons

**Mechanism:** When sellers know more than buyers (**information asymmetry / adverse selection**),
the market prices to the average quality, driving good assets out and leaving "lemons." Foundational
for understanding how information gaps create mispricing across whole categories.

**Application checklist:**
- Identify asymmetry: where does management/insiders know far more than the market? (complex
  accounting, opaque segments, EM disclosure gaps).
- Adverse-selection discounts: is a whole category (a sector, a market, post-spin orphans) priced as
  lemons, leaving good assets cheap?
- Signaling: do quality firms credibly signal (buybacks with insider buying, conservative accounting,
  clean disclosure) to separate from lemons?

**What it changes:** Explains category-level mispricing and identifies where credible signaling
distinguishes a good asset trapped in a discounted category.

**Failure modes:**
- Buying the cheap category without separating the good asset from the genuine lemons.
- Ignoring that the asymmetry may mean *you* are the less-informed party.

## Synthesis (sub-path of Path 2)

Consensus-driven edges run: **is consensus herded (Scharfstein-Stein)** → **is it lagging the latest
data (Abarbanell-Bernard)** → **is it ignoring accrual reversal (Bradshaw)** → **is the whole
category mispriced on asymmetry (Akerlof)**. Each identifies a specific reason the consensus number
is wrong and a catalyst (estimate revisions, accrual reversal, disclosure) that closes the gap.
