## 1. Jensen (1986) — Agency Costs of Free Cash Flow

**Mechanism:** Managers with abundant free cash flow and few good investment opportunities tend to
**destroy value** — empire-building acquisitions, pet projects, bloated cost structures — rather
than return cash to shareholders. Debt can be disciplining because it forces cash out. The lens for
all capital-allocation analysis.

**Application checklist:**
- Map FCF deployment over 5+ years: returned to shareholders (buybacks/dividends) vs. M&A vs. capex vs. cash hoarding.
- Judge M&A quality: strategic and accretive, or diversifying empire-building at premium prices?
- Excess cash + weak opportunities + acquisitive management = agency-cost red flag.
- Inverse signal: disciplined capital allocators with high incremental ROIC deserve a premium.

**What it changes:** Directly drives whether management is a value creator or destroyer in the risk
matrix; can justify a management-quality discount or premium in the valuation.

**Failure modes:**
- Reading a large cash pile as pure safety when it may signal coming value destruction.
- Crediting "growth" capex that earns below the cost of capital.

## 2. Jensen & Meckling (1976) — Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs, Ownership

**Mechanism:** The **principal-agent problem** is the DNA of governance. Managers (agents) and
shareholders (principals) have divergent interests; the gap generates agency costs. Ownership
structure and incentive alignment determine how large those costs are.

**Application checklist:**
- Assess incentive alignment: does management own meaningful equity? Is comp tied to per-share value
  and ROIC, or to size/revenue (which rewards empire-building)?
- Identify the principal-agent gaps specific to this firm: dispersed vs. concentrated ownership,
  insider control, board independence.
- Concentrated insider ownership cuts both ways: better alignment, but also entrenchment and
  minority expropriation risk (links to Tunneling).

**What it changes:** Frames every governance question as an incentive-alignment question, which is
more predictive than checklist-box governance scoring.

**Failure modes:**
- Equating insider ownership with good governance without checking for entrenchment.
- Ignoring comp structure that rewards size over per-share value.

## 3. Johnson, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer (2000) — Tunneling

**Mechanism:** Controlling shareholders extract value from a firm at the expense of minority
holders — "tunneling" assets or profits out via related-party transactions, transfer pricing,
intercorporate loans, dilutive issuance to insiders, or asset sales at non-arm's-length prices.
Essential for any promoter-led or family-controlled company, especially in emerging markets.

**Application checklist:**
- Scan filings for **related-party transactions**: loans to/from promoters, sales to affiliated
  entities, royalty/brand fees paid to the controlling family.
- Watch for value-extraction vectors: dilutive preferential allotments to insiders, asset transfers,
  pledged promoter shares.
- Group structure: complex holding-company webs are tunneling-enabling architecture.
- Minority protection: are minority holders structurally protected, or exposed?

**What it changes:** Can collapse a thesis entirely — a cheap promoter-led stock may be cheap
*because* minorities never see the cash (links back to Williams: value requires cash actually
reaching the shareholder).

**Failure modes:**
- Valuing a promoter-led name on consolidated earnings that minorities will never receive.
- Treating related-party transactions as boilerplate rather than reading them.

## 4. Kothari, Shu & Wysocki (2009) — Do Managers Disclose More Good News Than Bad?

**Mechanism:** Managers **delay bad news and accelerate good news**. Disclosure timing and tone are
strategically managed. This systematically biases the information flow an analyst reads.

**Application checklist:**
- Discount the timing: bad news released late (Friday, post-close, buried in filings) is worse than
  it looks; eagerly pre-announced good news may be the whole story.
- Read guidance credibility: chronic optimism, walk-downs, or selective disclosure patterns.
- Cross-check management tone against hard cash and accrual data (links to accounting-quality.md).

**What it changes:** Calibrates how much to trust management commentary and guidance; sharpens
reading of earnings-call tone.

**Failure modes:**
- Taking guidance at face value without a credibility track record.
- Missing that a slow, quiet bad-news release implies a bigger problem.

## Synthesis for Path 3

Governance assessment runs: **incentive alignment (Jensen-Meckling)** → **capital allocation
discipline (Jensen)** → **minority expropriation risk (Tunneling)** → **disclosure credibility
(Kothari)**. For EM and promoter-led names this is often the decisive layer — and it connects to
valuation first principles: if cash never reaches minorities, consolidated value is illusory. See
emerging-markets.md (Durnev-Kim) for sizing the governance premium.
