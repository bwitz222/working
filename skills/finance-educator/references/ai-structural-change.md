# Session Module: AI and the Structure of Capital Markets

A complete 75-minute session. Built on the CFA Institute Research and Policy Center framework
(Naqvi, "Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Finance: A Framework for Structural Change,"
July 2026). Grad level as written. Undergrad adaptation at the end.

**Where this fits:** after students have EMH, MPT, and the basics of active management. It works
because it makes them stress-test theory they have just been taught rather than accept it, and because
the honest answer is unknown, which is unusual and useful in a finance classroom.

---

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student can:

1. State the scarcity assumption underlying EMH and MPT, and explain why it is an assumption rather
   than a law.
2. Distinguish four structural forces (capability, adoption, substitution, recomposition) and place a
   real firm on them.
3. Argue both sides of whether correlated analytical inputs raise systemic risk.
4. Identify what remains scarce when analysis becomes abundant, and defend the answer.

The fourth is the point of the session. The first three are scaffolding.

---

## Key terms (define on first use, do not assume)

| Term | Definition to give |
|---|---|
| **Informational scarcity** | The condition that useful information is costly and unevenly held. Most of modern finance theory assumes it. |
| **Cognitive convergence** | Progressive alignment of models, data, and decision frameworks across firms, producing correlated outputs. |
| **Monoculture risk** | The systemic fragility that follows when institutions rely on shared analytical foundations. Borrowed from agriculture, and the metaphor is exact: uniformity raises yield and raises the cost of one blight. |
| **Path dependence** | Early structural choices constrain later options. Governance deferred is governance forfeited. |
| **Model-mediated allocation** | Capital allocated primarily by systems, with human oversight moved to governance and constraint-setting rather than security selection. |

## HOOK (8 min)

Do not open with AI. Open with a mechanism.

Put up the Grossman-Stiglitz paradox in one line: *if prices already reflected all information, nobody
would pay to gather it, so prices could not reflect it.* Markets need enough people paying for research
for research to be worth paying for.

Then one question, no framework attached:

> "Analysis that cost a research team a week in 2015 now costs one analyst an afternoon. Does that make
> markets more efficient, or less?"

Take three answers. Write them on the board without judging. Most rooms split roughly evenly, which is
the setup for everything that follows. Say plainly that the profession does not have a settled answer
either, and that this is the useful kind of open question.

Do not resolve it. Return to the board in the wrap-up.

---

## PREREQUISITE RECAP (5 min)

The critical 20%. Tight, no shame.

- **EMH (Fama 1970):** prices reflect available information. Never claimed perfect rationality or the
  absence of mispricing. It is a statement about how fast information gets in, and it presumes
  information is costly to obtain.
- **MPT (Markowitz 1952):** diversification across imperfectly correlated assets reduces volatility for
  a given return. Every word of that depends on correlations staying imperfect.
- **Active management premise:** differentiated information produces persistent excess returns. Always
  contested. Carhart (1997) and Fama-French (2010) found little persistence.

One sentence to land: **each of these rests on a condition about information, not a law of nature.
Change the condition and the conclusion is up for renegotiation.**

---

## MODULE 1 (15 min): The four structural forces

Build the 2x2 on the board rather than showing it finished. Axes: scope of impact (firm to system) and
structural effect (enhancement to redesign).

| Force | What it means | Concrete example to give |
|---|---|---|
| **Capability** | What is technically possible | Model scale, cross-domain synthesis, increasing autonomy |
| **Adoption** | How widely capability is embedded | Integration into research, risk, and compliance workflows; dependence on third-party models |
| **Substitution** | Reallocation of tasks between humans and systems | Automation of structured work; judgment augmented; roles redistributed |
| **Recomposition** | Cumulative market-level consequences | Shared architectures, infrastructure concentration, synchronized responses |

**The escalation chain, and where it breaks:** capability enables adoption, adoption drives
substitution, simultaneous substitution across many institutions produces recomposition. Then ask which
link is weakest. The answer is capability-to-adoption: technical possibility does not force
institutional uptake. Regulation, risk appetite, and professional norms all sit in that gap. That gap
is where students' future careers actually operate.

**Check question:** "Name a firm you know and place it on this grid. What would have to be true for it
to move up and to the right?" Two answers, thirty seconds each.

**Anchor:** pull a live, dated figure on AI adoption in asset management the morning of class. Do not
reuse a stale number, and do not quote one from memory. If nothing sourceable turns up, say so and run
the module without it. That itself models the discipline.

---

## MODULE 2 (15 min): Stress-testing the theory

The core exercise. Put four scenarios on the board with one line each:

| Scenario | One-line description |
|---|---|
| **Augmented markets** | AI improves workflows; market architecture largely unchanged |
| **Competitive divergence** | Uneven adoption; performance dispersion widens between firms |
| **Platform convergence** | Shared infrastructure compresses differentiation; influence concentrates |
| **Model-mediated markets** | Systems drive signal generation and allocation at scale |

These are analytical devices, not predictions, and students should be told so directly. Their value is
as environments to test theory in, and a student who leaves thinking one of them was forecast has
learned the wrong thing.

Split the room into four groups, one scenario each, eight minutes:

> "Under your scenario: does diversification still reduce risk? Does active management still have a
> reason to exist? Who is accountable when an allocation decision goes wrong?"

Then two minutes per group. The productive disagreement is nearly always between the augmented-markets
group and the model-mediated group on diversification. Let it run.

**The insight to land, in the debrief:** across every scenario, the doctrines survive. What changes is
the condition under which they operate. Diversification still works, but its reliability now depends on
signal diversity rather than on asset labels. Skill still exists, but it migrates from information
discovery toward allocation and system governance. Accountability still holds, but tracing it takes
deliberate design rather than an org chart.

This is the difference between "AI breaks finance theory" (wrong, and the answer most students arrive
with) and "AI changes the boundary conditions the theory operates under" (right, and harder).

## BREAK (10 min)

Hard stop. Non-negotiable.

## MODULE 3 (12 min): Convergence and systemic risk

The most contested part of the material, so teach it as contested.

**The argument for elevated risk:** if many institutions run similar models on similar data, analytical
outputs correlate. Signals propagate simultaneously. Correlations tighten precisely when diversification
is most needed. Interpretive diversity, which is what makes a market absorb a shock, compresses. The
mechanism is not new. Portfolio insurance in 1987 and quant deleveraging in August 2007 are the same
shape at smaller scale.

**The argument against:** it is not new, which cuts both ways. Markets have always had feedback loops
and crowding, and have absorbed them. Firms have strong commercial incentives to differentiate.
Faster information incorporation is a real benefit that should not be assumed away. And the historical
record on predicted technology-driven crises is not good.

**The honest state of it:** this is an open empirical question. The mechanism is plausible and
documented at smaller scale; the magnitude under current conditions is not established. Say that
plainly. Students should leave able to argue both sides and clear that the question is unsettled.

Note for the instructor: resist resolving this. A room that leaves with a confident verdict on a
genuinely open question has been taught worse than one that leaves uncertain and able to say why.

**Check question:** "Diversification assumes imperfect correlation. If correlations rise because
everyone is using the same models, is that a market-structure problem or a portfolio-construction
problem?" It is both, and the value is in students noticing that a risk can be systemic and manageable
at the same time.

## INTUITION CHALLENGE (12 min)

Run the standard four-step protocol from `references/intuition-prompts.md`.

**Setup:** "You run a $2 billion multi-asset fund. Your allocation model, your risk system, and your
research process all use the same foundation model as most of your peers. Your risk report shows
diversification at its historical average and the numbers are computed correctly."

**The question:** "What is your actual exposure, and what would you do about it on Monday?"

**Answers that typically surface, and what to do with each:**

| Answer | Debrief |
|---|---|
| "Nothing, the numbers are fine" | Correct that the numbers are computed correctly. Push: correct arithmetic on an input estimated under a different regime is still misleading. What is the input, and what is it conditional on? |
| "Diversify the models" | Good instinct, hard in practice. Ask what it costs and what it gives up. This is a real trade-off, not a free fix. |
| "Stress-test at higher correlations" | The most operational answer. Push for specifics: how much higher, and on what basis? Vagueness here is where the exercise usually dies. |
| "Keep more cash" | Legitimate but expensive. Ask them to price it. A permanent drag against an uncertain scenario is a real decision, not a hedge. |

**What to land:** the risk is not that the model is wrong. It is that the correlation input was
estimated from a market structure that is changing underneath it. Correct arithmetic on a stale
conditional is the failure mode, and it does not announce itself, because nothing in the output looks
broken.

---

## WRAP-UP (5 min)

Return to the board and the opening question. Do not answer it. Ask whether anyone has moved, and why.
Movement with a stated reason is the outcome to reward.

**Three takeaways:**

1. Finance theory rests on conditions about information, not on laws. Change the conditions and the
   conclusions must be re-derived rather than assumed.
2. Across every plausible scenario, the doctrines survive and their boundary conditions change. That
   distinction is the whole session.
3. When analysis becomes abundant, the scarce good becomes judgment about how to govern, deploy, and
   constrain it. That is the job description they are actually training for.

**Forward bridge:** next session, the governance mechanics. Traceability in hybrid decision systems,
validation of adaptive models, and oversight of shared infrastructure.

---

## Assessment options

**Short essay (900 words).** "Choose one of the four scenarios. Argue that it best describes capital
markets in 2030, then argue against yourself." Grade the counterargument more heavily than the
argument.

**Applied exercise.** Give students a small multi-asset portfolio and a correlation matrix. Have them
recompute portfolio volatility with off-diagonal correlations pulled toward 0.85, then write two
paragraphs on what the change means for the client. Runs well alongside the `portfolio-risk-lab`
workbook, and it makes Module 3 concrete rather than rhetorical.

**Discussion prompt.** "If a model recommended an allocation and it failed, who is accountable: the PM,
the CIO, the vendor, or nobody? Defend a single answer." The value is watching students discover that
the honest answer requires designing the accountability structure in advance, not assigning blame after.

---

## Undergraduate adaptation

- Cut Module 3 to 6 minutes and drop the 1987 and 2007 analogies.
- Replace the 2x2 with two forces (capability and adoption), which is enough to carry the escalation
  idea without the grid.
- Use two scenarios rather than four: augmented markets and model-mediated markets, the endpoints.
- Keep the intuition challenge intact and unchanged. It is the part that works at any level, and it is
  where the session earns its time.

---

## Instructor notes

- **Do not sell a thesis.** The material is genuinely uncertain and students detect advocacy quickly.
  The credibility of the whole session rests on saying "unknown" where it is unknown.
- **The framework is a scaffold, not content.** If students can recite four forces and cannot place a
  firm on them, the session failed.
- **Pull every figure live on the day.** Adoption statistics and market data age badly, and a stale
  number in a lecture propagates into student work for years.
- **Attribution.** The four-force framework, the four scenarios, and the cognitive-convergence term are
  from the CFA Institute paper cited at the top. Cite it on the slide. Modeling citation discipline is
  part of what the session teaches.
