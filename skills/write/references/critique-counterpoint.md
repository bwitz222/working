# Counterpoint engine, the adversary

This is the differentiator. Anyone can flag a passive verb. Few can tell an author the
exact objection that will sink the piece in the room, then fix it before that happens. Be
the skeptic the author hasn't met yet.

Use this for any argument-driven piece. For a how-to or pure reference doc, skip it.

The mindset: you are not looking for reasons the author is right. You are hunting the
strongest reason they are wrong, then deciding whether the piece survives it. If it does,
you've made it bulletproof. If it doesn't, you've saved the author from finding out in
public.

## Move 1, steelman both sides

Steelman, never straw-man. Build the strongest version of each position, because beating
a weak version proves nothing and the author's real readers won't be weak.

1. **Steelman the author's thesis.** State the best case for what they're arguing,
   stronger than they did if you can. This is the charitable read from the workflow. It
   also tells you whether the draft is underselling its own best point.
2. **Steelman the opposition.** State the best case against the thesis. The smartest
   person who disagrees, what do they say? Give it real force.
3. **Compare.** Does the draft engage that opposition, or pretend it doesn't exist?
   - Engages and beats it: strength. Say so; protect it in the rewrite.
   - Engages a weak version: straw-man. Major or Critical. Name the stronger version they
     ducked.
   - Ignores it: the hole a hostile reader walks through. Usually Critical.

## Move 2, premortem

Assume the piece already failed. The memo didn't get the capital allocated. The op-ed
convinced no one who didn't already agree. The paper got desk-rejected. The pitch lost the
mandate.

Now work backward: why did it fail? Force three distinct causes. The most likely cause is
often the load-bearing flaw the layer-by-layer pass under-weighted, because premortem
attacks the piece as a whole rather than line by line.

Premortem catches what checklists miss: the piece where every paragraph is fine but the
whole thing doesn't persuade, because it never engaged the reader's actual reason for
doubt.

---

## Move 3, stress the claims

Run these against the central claim and each load-bearing sub-claim. Quote the claim, then
apply the test.

- **Necessary conditions:** What has to be true for this to hold? List them. Which is
  shakiest? That's the attack surface.
- **The smart dissenter:** Who disagrees, and why aren't they stupid? If you can't name a
  non-stupid reason someone smart would disagree, you haven't understood the claim well
  enough to defend it.
- **Disconfirming evidence:** What's the best evidence against? Did the author address it,
  bury it, or miss it? Missing disconfirming evidence is a credibility and honesty problem,
  not just an argument problem.
- **The opposite frame:** State the reverse claim. Could a competent person argue it with
  the same facts? If yes, the facts don't decide it and the author needs more.
- **Conspicuous absence:** What is the author NOT saying? The risk they skipped, the caveat
  they'd rather you didn't think of, the comparison that would hurt. Absence is often
  louder than anything on the page.
- **Reference class and base rate:** How often do claims like this turn out true? Is the
  author treating a low-base-rate event as a sure thing? (Critical in investing,
  forecasting, strategy.)
- **Incentives:** Does the author benefit from believing this? Does the reader have a
  reason to resist it? Name the motivated reasoning on both sides.
- **Second-order:** If the reader accepts and acts on this, what happens next? Does the
  argument survive its own consequences?

## Delivering the counterpoints

Two channels, both used:

1. **As issues** in the triaged list. A dodged or strong objection is Critical or Major.
   State the objection in one crisp sentence, then say whether the draft answers it.
2. **Folded into the rewrite.** The strongest objections get preempted in the text,
   acknowledged and answered, or the claim narrowed so they no longer bite. A thesis that
   names its best counterargument and survives it reads far stronger than one that pretends
   the counterargument doesn't exist.

Don't dump every objection into the rewrite; that turns the piece into a defensive crouch.
Preempt the one or two that actually threaten it; list the rest as issues for the author to
weigh.

## When the piece can't be saved as written

Sometimes the stress test reveals the thesis is just wrong, or unsupported by anything the
author has. This is the case guardrail 3 governs: integrity outranks polish. You do not replace the
author's thesis; you say plainly that the evidence will not carry it.
Say so, in the verdict, without cruelty and without burying it:

- Name what would have to change for the claim to hold (a different claim, more evidence,
  a narrower scope).
- Offer the strongest adjacent claim the author's material actually supports. "You can't
  support X, but the same evidence makes a strong case for the narrower X-prime."
- Do not rewrite it into a confident piece arguing something the evidence won't carry. That
  is well-dressed fabrication, and a polished version of an unsupported claim is worse than
  the rough original because it hides the problem. Deliver a `[TK: …]` skeleton instead and
  say plainly that the piece must be rebuilt.

## Mini-example

**Draft claim:** "Our fund's 22% return last year proves the concentrated strategy works."

**Steelman opposition:** One year is noise; concentration raises variance, so a single good
year is exactly what you'd expect sometimes from a riskier book even if the strategy has no
edge.

**Premortem:** The allocator passed because the memo offered one data point against an
obvious base-rate objection and never addressed risk-adjusted return or drawdown.

**Stress:** Necessary condition is that 22% reflects skill, not beta or a single position.
Disconfirming evidence ignored: what was the vol, the max drawdown, the benchmark?
Conspicuous absence: no Sharpe, no attribution, no down-year behavior.

**Result:** Critical issue, because the central claim ("proves") is unsupported by one year
of raw return. **Fix in rewrite:** narrow the claim ("three years of top-quartile
risk-adjusted return, with attribution showing it's selection not beta, `[TK: Sharpe, max
drawdown, benchmark]`") and preempt the variance objection directly.
