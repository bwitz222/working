# Domain notes, where each kind of writing actually fails

Use the section that matches the piece. These are the failure modes a generalist editor
misses because they require knowing the form. They sharpen the diagnosis and aim the
counterpoint engine. Where a fact is missing, the fix is a visible [TK: ...] placeholder,
never an invented one.

## Contents
- Academic writing
- Persuasive and general prose
- Teaching and classroom materials

## Academic writing
Papers, abstracts, literature reviews, conference submissions, referee reports. Teaching
material has its own section at the bottom of this file.

**Claim-evidence rigor.** Every claim needs support, a citation, or an explicit "this is
conjecture." Strength of language must track strength of evidence: "demonstrates" vs.
"suggests" vs. "is consistent with." Overclaiming is the most common reviewer kill.

**Citation integrity.** Real, locatable sources. Never invent or approximate a citation;
flag `[TK: cite]`. A citation that doesn't support the claim it's attached to is worse than
none.

**Define terms before using them.** Jargon deployed before it's defined loses the reader
and hides imprecise thinking. Spot the term doing heavy lifting without a definition.

**Contribution must be clear.** For a paper: what's new here, and why does it matter to the
field? "Gap in the literature" is not enough; why is the gap worth filling?

**Method matches claim.** Does the evidence design actually support the conclusion?
Correlation presented as causation, a sample too small or unrepresentative, no control,
results that don't rule out the obvious alternative explanation.

**Pedagogical clarity.** Papers and lecture notes are judged differently. For anything a
student reads, the test is whether someone who does not already know this could follow it.
The classroom-specific failure modes have their own section below; use it rather than this
one whenever the piece is teaching material.

**Scope honesty.** State limitations and what the work does NOT show. A paper that
overclaims its scope invites the reviewer to do the limiting for it, unkindly.

## Persuasive and general prose
Op-eds, essays, blog posts, newsletters, manifestos, cover letters, marketing.

**So-what and stakes.** Why should the reader spend their attention? If the piece doesn't
answer this in the first lines, that's usually the load-bearing flaw.

**Steelman the other side.** Persuasion that straw-mans the opposition convinces only
people who already agree. The strongest version of the counterview must appear and be
answered. This is where most op-eds fail.

**Applause lights.** Sentences engineered to win nods without saying anything ("we must do
better," "innovation is key," "now more than ever"). They feel like content and aren't. Cut
or replace with a specific claim.

**Motte-and-bailey.** A bold headline claim defended, when pressed, by retreating to a
banal one. Watch for the thesis that shrinks under scrutiny. Pin the author to one claim.

**Concrete over abstract.** General assertions slide off the reader. The fix is almost
always a specific: a name, a number, a case, a scene. Vague pieces become good pieces
mostly by getting specific.

**Earned emotion.** Pathos the argument hasn't earned reads as manipulation. Emotion should
follow from the case, not substitute for it.

**Cover letters specifically.** The bar is "would this get a callback." The fatal flaw is
interchangeability, a letter any candidate could have sent. Demand specific evidence of fit
and a reason this person, this role. Cut the throat-clearing first paragraph almost every
time.

## Teaching and classroom materials
Lecture notes, slides, problem sets, exam questions, case walkthroughs, section handouts.
Graduate and undergraduate finance is the standing case. The reader is a student who lacks
the intuition the writer has forgotten acquiring, so the edit is run from their seat.

**Terms used before they are defined.** In finance the offender is usually a term with a
plain-English twin: basis, carry, duration, levered beta. The sentence reads fine to the
author and silently splits the room. Define at first use or drop the term.

**Examples that illustrate the writer's understanding.** A clean, symmetric example proves
the mechanism works; it does not touch the place students actually go wrong. Choose the
example that sits on the confusion: the negative cash flow, the mid-period date, the case
where the shortcut breaks.

**Worked examples that skip the hard step.** The step assumed obvious, sign conventions,
which discount rate, why this cash flow and not that one, is the exact step being taught.
"It follows that" in a solution is almost always a skipped step. Show the arithmetic that
students get wrong, not the algebra they can already do.

**Questions that test recall instead of transfer.** "Define WACC" checks memory; "the
target refinances at higher leverage, what happens to WACC and why" checks understanding. A
problem set answerable by formula matching teaches formula matching.

**Difficulty that jumps rather than ramps.** Map the prerequisite chain question by
question. If item three requires two new ideas at once, split it. Set the numbers so
arithmetic never becomes the obstacle, and flag any figure you cannot source as [TK: ...]
rather than inventing a plausible one.
