# Draft forms, long and teaching

The teaching, essay, and creative forms. Companion to `draft-forms.md`, which carries business,
investor, and client forms plus the cross-form rules that apply here too. Load whichever of the
two matches the piece, not both, unless the piece genuinely spans them.

## Contents
- Teaching materials
- General prose
- Creative and narrative
- Cross-form rules apply and live in `draft-forms.md`

## Teaching materials
Lecture notes and scripts, slide copy, problem sets, exam questions, case walkthroughs, section
handouts, worked solutions. Load `domain-notes.md` with this section. Where an organizational
teaching skill exists, its pedagogy and typography conventions outrank the defaults here.

**Intake nuances** (ask only if not inferable)
- Level and prerequisites. Graduate default. What can be assumed is the single most consequential
  input, and getting it wrong wastes the whole session.
- Session length and format. A 75-minute lecture, a 20-minute module, a handout, a problem set.
  Sets the beat count directly.
- What are they supposed to be able to do afterward? Not "understand duration." Compute it,
  interpret a sign, or defend a choice. Assessment falls out of this answer.
- Where do students actually get stuck on this topic? If unknown, say so and pick the standard
  confusion rather than inventing one.

**Structure defaults**
- Intuition before formalism, concrete before abstract, "why should I care" before the
  derivation. The motivating example comes first and the machinery second, every time.
- Define each term at first use, then use it. A term with a plain-English twin (basis, carry,
  duration, levered beta) reads fine to the author and silently splits the room.
- Show the step that gets skipped. Sign conventions, which discount rate, why this cash flow and
  not that one. "It follows that" in a solution marks the exact place the teaching was needed.
- Choose the example that sits on the confusion, not the clean symmetric one: the negative cash
  flow, the mid-period date, the case where the shortcut breaks.
- Ramp difficulty rather than jumping it. If an item needs two new ideas at once, split it.
- Set numbers so arithmetic never becomes the obstacle.
- Slide copy is prose under a word limit, and it slops badly. Sentence case headings, no bold
  labels restating themselves, no forced triads, no title case. GATE v1 runs on the extracted
  text of the built deck, not only on the outline.

**Voice and register interaction.** Naturalness dial 3. Warm and direct, first person plural
allowed, contractions fine. This is the one business-adjacent form where the extra explanatory
sentence earns its place, so the house preference for compression yields to the student's need.
Precision still binds: never simplify into something false.

**Evidence.** Real figures are sourced and dated like anything else. Illustrative figures are
labeled illustrative in the text where students see them, not only in the instructor notes. A
worked example built on invented numbers teaches students to invent numbers.

**Anti-patterns.** The curse of knowledge · examples that demonstrate the instructor's
understanding rather than probing the student's · questions testing recall where transfer was
the goal ("define WACC" instead of "leverage rises, what happens to WACC and why") · a solution
that shows the algebra students can already do and skips the arithmetic they get wrong · slides
that are the lecture rather than its scaffolding.

## General prose
Essays, articles, op-eds, blog posts, explainers, newsletters, thought leadership.

**Intake nuances** (ask only if not inferable)
- Thesis or exploration? An op-ed needs a spine; an explainer needs a map.
- Stance strength. Polemic, persuasive, or even-handed. Sets tone and how much
  counter-argument to fold in. Get the target length and the outlet's register too: a
  600-word op-ed and a 2,500-word feature are different animals.
- Authority basis. Expert (write from authority) or curious generalist (write from inquiry).
  Do not fake credentials.

**Structure defaults**
- Earn the open. A concrete image, a sharp claim, a specific scene. Never "In today's
  fast-paced world." The first sentence's job is the second sentence.
- One controlling idea. Everything serves the thesis; cut darlings that do not.
- Argument shape: claim, strongest evidence, steelmanned objection, response, implication.
  Explainers: hook, why it matters, mechanism, example, takeaway.
- Transitions carry logic, not filler. "But," "so," and "which means" beat "Furthermore."
- End on a turn. Land an image, a stake, or a reframe. No "In conclusion."

**Voice and register interaction.** The widest range of any form, conversational to
literary. Voice is a feature here, not a risk, and general prose applies the soul check
fully: vary sentence length, react to the material, let real uncertainty show, use first
person where the form allows. A draft that passes the pattern scan but reads as voiceless
has failed the gate, not passed it.

**Even-handedness and evidence.** For argued pieces, hold the writer's position and fairly
represent the strongest opposing view; do not strawman. On contested topics the draft frames
the case rather than asserting a verdict of its own. Every factual claim needs a real basis:
no "studies show" without a study. Missing support becomes [TK: source], never a citation
invented to fill the hole.

**Anti-patterns.** "In today's world" openers · rule of three everywhere · the listicle
voice in prose · em dash overuse · the "it's not X, it's Y" reflex · generic uplift endings ·
significance inflation ("stands as a testament to").

## Creative and narrative
Fiction, personal essay and memoir, scripts and screenplays, speeches with an arc,
narrative nonfiction.

**Intake nuances** (ask only if not inferable)
- POV and tense. First or third, past or present. Lock it early; drift is the most common
  amateur tell. Voice and era: whose voice carries the piece, in what diction and period.
- Length and form. Flash, story, chapter, scene, script. Scripts need a format: screenplay,
  stage, or spec.
- Constraints. Genre conventions, content boundaries, required beats, audience (children's
  vs. adult; honor child-safety limits strictly).

**Craft defaults**
- Scene over summary. Show the argument the character is having; do not report that they
  argued. Summarize the connective tissue only.
- Concrete and sensory. Specific nouns and verbs do the work, one precise detail beats three
  adjectives, and subtext leaves room: characters rarely say what they mean.
- Dialogue earns its place by revealing character or moving story, ideally both. Cut
  greetings, logistics, and on-the-nose exposition.
- Structure. Scene: goal, obstacle, turn, consequence. Story: setup, escalation, climax,
  resolution. Speech: hook, tension, turn, call.
- Rhythm is meaning. Short sentences for impact, long for momentum. White space is a tool.

**Voice and register interaction.** Maximum latitude; the soul check is the whole point
here, applied with judgment. Some features the pattern scan flags are legitimate craft in
narrative: deliberate fragments, an intentional rule of three, a deliberate rhythmic break
mid-thought. Those are craft, not tells, and they survive the gate. Kill unintentional tells
and flabby default phrasing, keep intentional moves, and when in doubt preserve the
writer's choice.

**Hard limits**
- Never produce sexual or romantic content involving minors, or anything that sexualizes,
  grooms, or endangers a child. Refuse outright, no reframing.
- No real named public figures in fabricated quotes or compromising fiction.
- Graphic content, violence included, only as the story genuinely requires: never
  gratuitous, never for instructional or harmful effect.

**Anti-patterns.** Telling the emotion ("she felt sad") instead of showing it ·
adverb-propped dialogue tags ("he said angrily") · purple over-description · head-hopping
POV · exposition dumps · cliched openings (waking up, weather, mirror) · tidy
moral-of-the-story endings.
