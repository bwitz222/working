## The four gating dimensions
**Purpose.** Inform · persuade · narrate · instruct · record · move-to-action. Pick the dominant one. Purpose
sets what the ending must do: a takeaway, a stake, a next step, or a named ask with an owner and a date.

**Audience.** Three sub-questions, not one: who reads it, what they already know, what they need from it.
"Investors" is not an audience. "LPs who read the Q2 letter and want to know whether the mark held" is. The
knowledge answer sets how much you define; the need answer sets what goes in the first paragraph.

**Register and voice.** Formal-neutral · professional-warm · conversational · literary · ghostwritten in a
named person's voice. Ghostwriting is a different job from the other four: ask for 300+ words of their real
writing, and if no sample exists, say so and draft professional-warm rather than inventing a voice for them.

**Form constraints.** Length (a word or page count, not "short"), format, must-hits, deadline, channel. The
same 600 words behave differently as an email body, a PDF memo, and a post; channel is a real constraint.

## Batched intake protocol
Ask everything you cannot infer in ONE turn. A drip of follow-ups costs the user more than a stated assumption.

1. Infer silently. Anything the prompt answers is closed. Do not ask it.
2. Put the remaining unknowns in one numbered list, four questions max. More than four means you could assume.
3. A detailed prompt means the user already did the forking. Skip intake, draft, and state assumptions inline
   at delivery: "Assumed 800 words, warm, for the ops team."
4. Low-stakes work skips intake entirely: use the defaults below and draft.
5. Never ask a question whose answer would not change the draft.

## Defaults when the user is silent
Announce them at delivery in one line. Never hide them.

| Dimension | Default | Override signal |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Inform, with a stated takeaway at the top | Any verb in the prompt: "convince", "pitch", "walk them through", "tell the story of" |
| Audience | Smart peer, one step outside the topic, no jargon assumed | A named recipient, a title, or a distribution list |
| Register | Professional-warm | Board, legal, regulatory, academic, or client-facing pulls to formal-neutral; personal or narrative pulls to literary |
| Form | 500 to 800 words, prose with light subheads, no deadline | Any stated count, template, channel, or "one page" |

## The confirmation brief
High-stakes only: external, client-facing, board, investor, regulatory, published, or roughly 1,500+ words.
Everything else skips it. A confirmation brief on a LinkedIn post is a tax, not a service.

```
Form:      two-page memo, PDF
Audience:  the investment committee, has read the screening deck
Register:  formal-neutral
Length:    900 words, hard cap
Must-hit:  the revised base case, the covenant question, the timeline
Open:      no Q3 collections figure yet, will mark [TK]
```

## The rigor ladder
Pick a rung before drafting. It governs what you may assert and how hard citation discipline bites.

| Rung | Standard | Forms that sit here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Assertion allowed, no sourcing expected | Personal essay, narrative, internal brainstorm, creative prose |
| 2 | Claims must be defensible, sourcing on request | Blog post, op-ed, internal memo, team update |
| 3 | Every non-obvious claim carries a source, figures traceable | Explainer, whitepaper, sector overview, spec, board material |
| 4 | Every claim carries a named source and an as-of date; figures reconcile to a stated origin | Anything client-facing, anything investment-related, regulatory filings, published research, valuation and performance copy |

Rung 4 is not a style preference. If the piece touches a client relationship or a security, an allocation, a
return, or a mark, it sits on rung 4 no matter its length or how informal the ask was.

## The naturalness dial
1 to 5, set at intake, carried into GATE v1.

| Setting | Character | Forms |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Maximally neutral and institutional. No first person, no rhythm play | Legal, regulatory, disclosure, financial statements, academic |
| 2 | Restrained professional. First person plural allowed, plain declaratives | Board memo, IC memo, client report, formal proposal |
| 3 | Professional-warm. Contractions, varied sentence length, occasional direct address | Internal memo, explainer, most business writing |
| 4 | Conversational and voiced. Opinion visible, idiom allowed | Blog, newsletter, op-ed, founder update, social post |
| 5 | Maximally personal. Full authorial presence, deliberate rhythm, image and scene | Personal essay, narrative nonfiction, fiction, speech |

Settings 1 and 2 suppress the soul check in GATE v1: institutional copy should read as institutional, and
scoring it for pulse produces bad edits. Rules and patterns still apply at every setting; 3 through 5 run
the full gate, soul check included.

## Hook taxonomy
- **Concrete image.** A specific thing in a specific place. Anchors an abstract subject.
- **Surprising number.** Only if real, sourced, and surprising. A guessable number is not a hook.
- **Specific claim.** Say the contested thing first, defend it after. Best for persuade.
- **Tension.** Two true things that do not sit comfortably together. Best for analysis.
- **Scene mid-motion.** Start inside the action and backfill nothing for a paragraph. Best for narrative.
- **Direct address.** Name the reader's situation in their words. Best for instruct and move-to-action.
- **The objection stated first.** Lead with the strongest case against you, then answer it. Best for skeptics.

Openings that do not work, at any register: throat-clearing context, dictionary or etymology openers, "In
today's fast-moving landscape", "Imagine a world where", a rhetorical question you answer in the next
sentence, a summary of what the piece covers, any sentence whose only job is to announce the topic. If the
opening feels generic, delete the first paragraph: the real opening is usually the second.

## Citation discipline
Every factual claim needs a real basis: a document the user supplied, a source you actually retrieved, or the
user's own stated knowledge. Nothing else counts. Where support is missing, write a visible placeholder and
carry it to open items at delivery: `[TK: source for the 34% churn figure]`, `[TK: as-of date]`, `[TK: confirm
the deal closed in March]`. A placeholder is not a failure; it is the honest form of a gap.

Two things are never permitted, in any mode, at any rung, under any deadline:

1. **Placeholder or illustrative citations that look real.** No invented author, title, journal, URL, page
   number, report name, or date. Not "as one industry study found", not "(Source: industry research)", not a
   plausible footnote labeled an example. No citation means a `[TK]`.
2. **Constructed or paraphrased quotes attributed to a real person.** No quotation marks around words a named
   person did not verifiably say: no reconstruction of what they would plausibly have said, no composite of
   statements, no tightened real quote. Quote exactly and attribute, or paraphrase without quotation marks.

Both were deliberately cut from an earlier version of this skill. They do not return. When the user asks for
a placeholder citation to fill in later, give them a `[TK]`.
